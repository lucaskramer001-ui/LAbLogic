We spend about a third of every day asleep. So why don't we just stop sleeping and get those hours back? In 1964, 17-year-old Randy Gardner stayed awake for 264 hours (11 days), and it showed what happens when you try.

Gardner's body held up, but his mind didn't: his memory slipped, he became irritable, and one observer reported hallucinations. Guinness World Records no longer accepts attempts at the record because of the danger. You don't need 11 days to feel it: after about 17 hours awake, impairment is comparable to a blood alcohol level of 0.05%, and after 24 hours to 0.10%, above the US legal driving limit of 0.08%. Sleep also does real work: in mice, the space between brain cells grows by about 60% during sleep, letting fluid wash out waste, including beta-amyloid, a protein linked to Alzheimer's. And rats kept completely awake in lab experiments were all dying within about 11 to 32 days.
