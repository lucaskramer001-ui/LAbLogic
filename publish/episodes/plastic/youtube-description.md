Only about 9% of the world's plastic waste ends up recycled. But in 2016 scientists found a bacterium that eats plastic. So why don't we just spread it on every landfill and let it eat everything?

The bacterium, Ideonella sakaiensis, was found at a PET bottle recycling site in Japan. It uses two enzymes, PETase and MHETase, to cut PET plastic into pieces small enough to eat. But it's slow: in the lab it took about six weeks at 30 °C to almost completely break down a thin film of PET. It only eats PET, the plastic in drink bottles. Bags and wrappers are made of other plastics, and there is no enzyme yet that breaks those down well. The pile is huge: the world made about 460 million tonnes of plastic in 2019. And the bacterium breathes out much of what it eats: in the study, about three quarters of the plastic's carbon became CO2.

So scientists moved the idea into a factory. The French company Carbios engineered a faster enzyme that breaks down about 90% of PET in roughly 10 hours, and the pieces can be made into new bottles.
