After a plane crash, investigators search the wreckage for one thing: the black box. It's built to survive almost anything. So why don't we just build the whole plane out of it?

First, the black box isn't black. It's bright orange, so searchers can spot it.

And it isn't made of some magic material. The part that has to survive is small: a few memory chips. They sit inside thick layers of insulation, wrapped in a shell of titanium or stainless steel. One maker's armored memory unit weighs about two kilograms, just to protect a handful of chips.

That armor is tested brutally. It has to survive an impact of three thousand four hundred g, a fire at eleven hundred degrees Celsius for a full hour, and being crushed under more than two tonnes.

Now scale that up to a whole airliner. A plane is mostly empty space for people, bags and fuel. Wrap all of it in armor that thick, and it gets far too heavy to ever take off.

And even if it did, it wouldn't save anyone inside. An Air Force doctor once survived about forty-six g for a split second. Three thousand four hundred g is far beyond what any human body can take.

The black box doesn't make crashes survivable. It makes them understandable, so the next flight is safer.
