You could cover twenty percent of the Sahara in solar panels, and the Amazon could lose rain. So why don't we just do it anyway?

The Sahara is huge, it's the sunniest place on Earth, and almost nobody lives there. Fill it with panels, and the world has clean power. Easy, right?

The first problem is the cable. The power has to reach the cities. Desertec, a plan to send Sahara power to Europe, fell apart in twenty thirteen. And in twenty twenty-five, Britain turned down a four thousand kilometer cable from Morocco.

The second problem is sand. Dust storms coat the panels. In just one month, dust can cut their power by up to thirty percent. And cleaning them takes water. In a desert.

The third problem is the weirdest. Panels are dark, and the sand is bright. In a twenty twenty-one climate model, covering twenty percent of the Sahara made the desert about one and a half degrees hotter. The rain belt shifted north, and the Amazon got drier. Even the whole planet warmed, by about zero point one six degrees.

So the answer isn't one giant solar farm. It's lots of smaller ones, close to the people who use them.

The sun is free. Moving it isn't.
