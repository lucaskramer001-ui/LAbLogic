Governments run short of money all the time. They have bills to pay, workers to pay, and debts to pay back. And every government has something no one else has. A printing press.

So here's the obvious fix. Print more money. Hand everyone a stack of cash, and poverty is solved.

It sounds too good to be true. And it is. But the reason is surprisingly simple, and it has nothing to do with paper or ink.

Let's start with what money actually is.

Money isn't wealth. You can't eat a banknote, live in it, or drive it to work. Money is a ticket. It's a ticket you trade for real things, like bread, shoes, fuel and somebody's time.

The real wealth is the stuff. And printing more tickets doesn't bake a single extra loaf.

Picture a tiny town. It has ten loaves of bread, and all the people together have a hundred dollars. If everyone spends their money on bread, each loaf ends up costing about ten dollars.

Now the town prints another hundred dollars and hands it out. Everyone feels richer. But there are still only ten loaves.

So what happens? People line up with more money than before, and the baker notices. The price climbs toward twenty dollars a loaf. After all that printing, nobody can buy more bread than before.

That's inflation. More money chasing the same amount of stuff.

Printing isn't the only thing that pushes prices up. A bad harvest or more expensive oil can do it too, because then there's less stuff to go around. But the amount of money is something a government and its central bank control directly.

And printing isn't even fair. Whoever gets the new money first can spend it before prices rise. Wages often catch up later. And anyone with savings sitting in the bank pays the higher prices with money that's now worth less.

Now push that idea much further.

Economists call it hyperinflation when prices rise more than fifty percent in a single month. And history has run this experiment more than once.

Germany, 1923. After the First World War, Germany owed huge reparation payments. In early 1923, French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr, Germany's industrial heart, because payments had fallen behind.

German workers there stopped working in protest. And the government paid for that passive resistance by printing money.

At the end of 1922, a loaf of bread in Berlin cost around a hundred and sixty marks. By late 1923, it cost around two hundred billion marks.

People's savings, built up over a lifetime, became worthless.

The crisis ended in November 1923 with a new currency, the Rentenmark. One new Rentenmark was worth one trillion old marks.
