You might think the world learned its lesson. It didn't.

Zimbabwe, 2008. From the year 2000, the government seized large commercial farms, and farm output collapsed. The country was producing far less, and the government kept printing money to pay its bills.

Fewer goods. More money. You already know where that goes.

By 2007, prices were rising so fast that the government tried the next obvious fix. In June 2007, it ordered shops to cut their prices roughly in half. Police arrested thousands of shop owners and managers who didn't comply.

Within days, bread, milk, meat and cornmeal disappeared from the shelves. Selling at those prices meant selling at a loss, so shops ran out and weren't restocked. A low price on an empty shelf doesn't feed anyone.

So the printing went on.

In November 2008, economists estimate prices in Zimbabwe were doubling about every twenty-five hours. Imagine the price of your groceries doubling every day.

In January 2009, the central bank issued a one hundred trillion dollar note. A few months later, Zimbabwe gave up on its own currency and started using US dollars and other foreign money instead.

And it still isn't the record. That belongs to Hungary in 1946, right after the Second World War. At the peak, prices there doubled about every fifteen hours.

Look at these stories together and you see the same pattern. The amount of real stuff shrank or stalled, and the amount of money exploded.

So should we never print money at all? Not quite.

Printing money does have a job. When an economy grows, people make more things and do more work. If the amount of money never grew with it, prices would slowly fall. That sounds nice, but falling prices can make people delay spending and make debts harder to pay.

That's why central banks add money carefully. The US Federal Reserve, for example, aims for inflation of about two percent a year over the long run. Prices rise a little, slowly and predictably.

The trouble starts when money grows much faster than the things it can buy.

So why don't we just print more money?

Because money is a ticket, not the treasure. Print more tickets, and each ticket buys less. Push it too far, and a loaf of bread can cost two hundred billion.

More money doesn't make more bread. It just makes bread cost more.
