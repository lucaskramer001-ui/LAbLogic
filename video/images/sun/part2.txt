Now, let's add the trash.

Say we only wanted to get one year of the world's trash into low Earth orbit. Not to the Sun. Just a few hundred kilometers up.

SpaceX's Falcon Heavy is one of the most powerful rockets flying today. In its strongest setup, it can lift about sixty-four tonnes to low Earth orbit.

Two billion tonnes, sixty-four tonnes at a time, is about thirty-one million launches. For one year of trash. That's around eighty-six thousand launches every single day.

For comparison, the entire world attempted about two hundred and sixty orbital launches in 2024. That was a record.

And remember, that's only to orbit. A rocket heading for the Sun could carry just a small part of that load.

Then there's the rockets themselves. They're far more reliable than they used to be, but launches still fail sometimes.

A rocket full of trash that breaks apart on the way up doesn't make the trash disappear. It spreads it across the sky, the sea, or the land below.

This isn't a new idea, either. In the 1970s and early 1980s, NASA studied launching one special kind of waste into space. Nuclear waste. It's small in volume and dangerous for a very long time, so if any trash was worth the effort, it was this.

Even then, a 1973 study found that sending waste straight into the Sun wasn't possible with any of the rockets they considered. It simply took too much energy. They looked at pushing it out of the solar system instead.

NASA's reports named the two biggest questions: safety and cost. And a 1981 report estimated that handling just eight tonnes of waste a year would take three Space Shuttle launches.

In the end, nuclear waste never went to space.

So why don't we just fire our trash into the Sun?

Because everything that leaves Earth is already flying sideways at thirty kilometers per second. Getting rid of that speed takes enormous amounts of energy, years of careful flying, and a launch about every second that never fails.

The real trash problem is down here. The World Bank estimates that a third or more of the world's waste isn't managed properly. It's dumped, or burned in the open.

Collecting it, recycling it and burying it safely is a lot less exciting than a rocket to the Sun. But it's the fix that actually works.

The Sun looks close enough to aim at every single day. It's just a lot harder to hit than it looks.
