Every year, humanity throws away about two billion tonnes of trash. That's the World Bank's estimate, and it expects the number to climb to three point four billion tonnes a year by 2050.

Landfills fill up. Some of it gets burned in the open. Some of it ends up in rivers and in the ocean.

Meanwhile, about a hundred and fifty million kilometers away, there's a star that could swallow anything we throw at it.

So here's the obvious fix. Load the trash onto rockets, aim them at the Sun, and let it burn.

It sounds simple. But the Sun is one of the hardest places to reach in the whole solar system. And the reason has nothing to do with the heat.

Let's start with where we are. Earth isn't sitting still. It's racing around the Sun at about thirty kilometers per second. That's roughly sixty-seven thousand miles per hour.

And here's the part that's easy to miss. Everything on Earth is moving with it. You, your house, your trash, and every rocket sitting on a launch pad.

So when a rocket leaves Earth, it doesn't start from zero. It starts out already flying sideways around the Sun at thirty kilometers per second.

That's also why Earth doesn't fall into the Sun. In a way, it is falling, all the time. It's just moving sideways so fast that it keeps missing. That's what an orbit is.

Picture yourself on a fast train. You drop a ball out of the window. It doesn't fall straight down next to you. It keeps the train's speed and flies forward with you.

Trash launched from Earth does the same thing. It doesn't drop toward the Sun. It just keeps circling it, right alongside us.

To actually hit the Sun, a rocket has to cancel that sideways speed. Not speed up. Slow down. By a lot.

So how hard is that? NASA found out with the Parker Solar Probe.

Parker launched in 2018 to fly closer to the Sun than anything ever had. It didn't even need to hit the Sun. But NASA says it still had to get rid of about fifty-three thousand miles per hour of sideways speed.

NASA puts it this way. Reaching the Sun takes fifty-five times more energy than reaching Mars.

A powerful rocket wasn't enough on its own. So Parker borrowed help from Venus. It swung past Venus seven times, and every pass slowed it down a little more.

It took six years. On Christmas Eve 2024, Parker passed about three point eight million miles from the Sun's surface, moving at around four hundred and thirty thousand miles per hour. No spacecraft has ever come closer.

And that leads to a strange fact. It's actually easier to throw something out of the solar system completely than to drop it into the Sun.

Starting from Earth's orbit, escaping the Sun's pull takes about twelve extra kilometers per second. Falling straight into the Sun means taking away almost all of Earth's thirty.
