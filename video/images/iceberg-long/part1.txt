In early 2018, Cape Town was weeks away from turning off its taps. The city called it Day Zero. Four million people would have had to line up for water at collection points.

Meanwhile, about four thousand kilometers to the south, Antarctica keeps breaking off icebergs. Giant ones. And an iceberg is fresh water. It's made from snow that fell on land and was pressed into ice over thousands of years.

So here's the obvious fix. Send a few ships south, tie a rope around an iceberg, and tow it to the city.

It sounds a little crazy. But people have taken this idea seriously for about fifty years. And the reason it hasn't happened has nothing to do with ships.

Let's start in 1977. A Saudi prince, Mohammed al-Faisal, wanted fresh water for the Arabian Peninsula. He helped pay for an international conference on icebergs at Iowa State University. To get attention, a chunk of glacier ice was shipped from Alaska all the way to Iowa.

The prince's company planned to tow an Antarctic iceberg of about a hundred million tonnes to Arabia. In 1977, he was quoted saying it could happen within three years.

It never happened. And the first problem is the part of the iceberg you can't see.

About ninety percent of an iceberg sits below the waterline. The bit you see on top is only the tip.

Researchers at the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution ran computer simulations of towing icebergs. They found that an iceberg big enough to survive the trip had to be at least three hundred meters long and about two hundred meters thick.

Two hundred meters. That's about as tall as a fifty-story building, mostly hanging under the surface.

So the iceberg can't come into a harbor. It would hit the sea floor long before it reached the coast. It has to be parked far out at sea, and the water has to travel the rest of the way.

Then there's the tow itself. Ships already tow icebergs today, off the coast of Newfoundland in Canada. Not to drink them. To push them away from offshore oil platforms.

And those crews know how tricky it is. Icebergs are smooth and slippery, so the rope can slide right off. And because most of the weight sits deep underwater, pulling on the top can make the whole iceberg roll over.

Now picture doing that for weeks.

In the Woods Hole simulations, the tow moved at about half a meter per second. That's slower than a slow walk. At that speed, reaching Cape Town took about two months.
