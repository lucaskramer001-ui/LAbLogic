And for the whole trip, the iceberg is melting.

Near Antarctica, the sea is close to freezing. But the closer you get to Cape Town, the warmer it gets. The sea off Cape Town is around fifteen to twenty degrees Celsius. That's roughly sixty to sixty-eight degrees Fahrenheit.

Warm water eats away at the ice from below. Waves wear down the sides. Pieces crack off and float away.

That's why smaller icebergs in the study didn't make it at all. They melted before they arrived.

But the real trouble starts when the trip ends.

Once the iceberg is parked off the coast, it sits in that warm water day and night. The study found that a parked iceberg could melt in weeks, or even days.

So how big would it need to be? To give Cape Town a full year of water, without anything to slow the melting, the iceberg would have to be about three kilometers long when it arrives.

Three kilometers. And it still has to start much bigger than that, because so much is lost on the way.

People have suggested wrapping the iceberg in a giant skirt of fabric to shield it from the warm water. That could help. But nobody has tested it on an iceberg that size.

And that leads to the hardest problem of all. Even if the iceberg arrives, how do you get the water out?

When ice melts in the sea, the fresh water doesn't stay in a neat puddle. It mixes into the salt water around it. And once it's mixed, you're back to salty water.

You could try to carve off blocks, or pump the meltwater up before it mixes. But nobody has a proven way to do that at this scale.

In 2018, during the drought, a salvage expert named Nick Sloane proposed exactly this plan for Cape Town. He estimated one big iceberg could supply about a fifth to a third of the city's water for a year.

By his own estimate, towing it would cost about a hundred million dollars, plus tens of millions more to collect the water.

The city said no. Officials said groundwater and desalination were cheaper, or at least no more expensive, and far less risky.

So what did Cape Town actually do? People saved water. Short showers. No watering gardens. A limit of fifty liters per person per day. The city cut its water use roughly in half.

Then the rain came back. Day Zero never arrived.

So why don't we just tow an iceberg?

Because most of it hides underwater, so it can't come near the shore. Because it's slippery, top-heavy, and slow to drag. Because it melts the whole way there, and even faster once it's parked. And because nobody knows how to catch the water before it disappears into the sea.

The ice is free. Getting it into a tap is the hard part.
