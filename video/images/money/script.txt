If a country is short on money, why doesn't it just print more? Hand everyone a stack of cash, and poverty is solved.

Here's the catch. Money isn't wealth. It's a ticket for buying real things, like bread, shoes and fuel. Printing more tickets doesn't bake a single extra loaf.

Picture a town with ten loaves of bread and a hundred dollars. Each loaf costs about ten dollars. Now print another hundred dollars and hand it out. Everyone feels richer, but there are still only ten loaves. So the price climbs toward twenty dollars a loaf, and nobody can buy more than before.

Push that far enough and you get hyperinflation.

In Germany, a loaf of bread in Berlin cost around a hundred and sixty marks at the end of 1922. By late 1923, it cost around two hundred billion marks.

Zimbabwe tried it more recently. In November 2008, economists estimate prices there were doubling about every twenty-five hours. The central bank ended up printing a one hundred trillion dollar note.

Printing money does have a job. Central banks add some as an economy grows and makes more stuff. The trouble starts when money grows much faster than the things it can buy.

More money doesn't make more bread. It just makes bread cost more.
