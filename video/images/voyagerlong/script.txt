You could call Voyager 1 today, but your hello would take a full day to arrive. And in nineteen seventy-seven, it almost never left Earth.

Today it's forty-nine years old. It's running on less than half of its original power, and it has two working science instruments left. So why don't we just switch it off?

To answer that, you need the whole story. And it starts with planets lining up.

In the mid nineteen-sixties, a young engineer at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, Gary Flandro, was working out routes to the outer planets. He noticed something rare. In the late nineteen-seventies, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune would all sit on the same side of the Sun, lined up like stepping stones. That only happens about once every hundred and seventy-five years.

Why does that matter? Because a planet can throw a spacecraft. Fly close behind Jupiter as it moves, and its gravity pulls you along and flings you out faster, like a skateboarder grabbing the back of a passing bus. Planet after planet, each one hands the spacecraft on to the next.

Without that trick, a trip to Neptune would take about forty years. With it, less than ten. Miss the window, and you wait more than a century and a half. NASA didn't miss it.

NASA built two identical spacecraft, Voyager 1 and Voyager 2. Each one is about the size of a small car, with a big white dish antenna pointed at home. The official mission was Jupiter and Saturn, and it was planned to last about five years.

Their computers had about sixty-nine kilobytes of memory. A single photo on your phone is bigger than that.

There are no solar panels, because out there, sunlight is far too weak. Instead, each Voyager carries a nuclear battery. Plutonium gives off heat, and the heat is turned into electricity. It never runs out suddenly. It just slowly fades.

And bolted to the side, each one carries a gold-plated record. A committee led by the astronomer Carl Sagan filled it with pictures, sounds of Earth, and greetings in fifty-five languages. There's whale song, thunder, a heartbeat. There's Bach, Beethoven, and Chuck Berry's Johnny B. Goode. Some people on the committee thought rock and roll was too adolescent. Sagan's answer was that there are a lot of adolescents on this planet. It went on the record. Just in case someone, someday, finds it.

Voyager 2 launched first, on August twentieth, nineteen seventy-seven. Voyager 1 followed on September fifth. It got the number one because it was on a faster path, and it would reach Jupiter first.

That launch almost went wrong. One stage of the Titan rocket underperformed, so the Centaur upper stage had to burn longer to make up for it. When it was done, according to the mission's planning manager, it had about three and a half seconds of fuel left.

Three and a half seconds. That was the first time Voyager almost didn't make it. It wasn't the last.

In April nineteen seventy-eight, Voyager 2's main radio receiver failed. The backup had a broken part too. It could still hear Earth, but only on one very narrow frequency, and that frequency drifts with the spacecraft's temperature and motion.

So engineers learned to aim. For every command since then, they have worked out exactly where Voyager 2 will be listening. For forty-eight years, it has been flying on its backup receiver.

In March nineteen seventy-nine, Voyager 1 reached Jupiter. The pictures were stunning: storms bigger than Earth, and the Great Red Spot swirling like paint. It even found that Jupiter has thin, faint rings.

But the biggest surprise came from a navigation engineer, Linda Morabito. She was checking images used to steer the spacecraft, and she saw a strange cloud at the edge of Jupiter's moon Io. It was a plume about three hundred kilometers high. A volcano, erupting. The first active volcano ever found beyond Earth. Scientists had expected Io to be a dead, cratered moon. Instead, it was the most active world they had ever seen.

Next came Saturn, in November nineteen eighty. Here, Voyager 1 faced a choice. Saturn's biggest moon, Titan, has a thick atmosphere, and scientists badly wanted a close look. But flying past Titan would bend Voyager 1's path up and out of the plane of the planets. No Uranus. No Neptune.

They chose Titan. Voyager 1's tour of the planets ended right there.

So the grand tour fell to Voyager 2. It reached Uranus in January nineteen eighty-six, and Neptune in August nineteen eighty-nine. At Neptune, it measured the strongest winds in the solar system, and on its moon Triton, it caught dark plumes shooting up from the ice, like geysers. It is still the only spacecraft that has ever visited either of these planets. Every close-up picture of those two worlds comes from a few days of Voyager 2 flying past.

By nineteen ninety, Voyager 1 was about six billion kilometers from the Sun. Carl Sagan pushed for one last picture: turn the camera around, and photograph home.

On February fourteenth, nineteen ninety, it did. In that picture, Earth is smaller than a single pixel. A pale blue dot, caught in a beam of scattered sunlight.

Thirty-four minutes later, Voyager 1's cameras were switched off for good, to save power for the long road ahead.

The Sun blows a constant wind of charged particles. That wind carves out a huge bubble around the planets, called the heliosphere. Where the bubble ends, interstellar space begins.

In August twenty-twelve, Voyager 1 crossed that edge. But nobody could say so for sure. The instrument built to measure the solar wind directly had stopped working back in nineteen eighty. It took more than a year, and a burst of activity from the Sun that made the gas around Voyager ring like a bell, before scientists were confident. Voyager 1 was in interstellar space. Voyager 2 followed in November twenty-eighteen. They are the only working spacecraft that have ever measured interstellar space directly. Out there, there is no map. Voyager is drawing it.

Fixing a spacecraft you can never touch is hard. Fixing one that is almost a full day of light travel away is something else.

In July twenty twenty-three, a wrong command tilted Voyager 2's antenna two degrees away from Earth. Two degrees, and it went silent. The giant dish in Canberra, Australia, sent its strongest signal, an interstellar shout, telling the spacecraft to turn back. The message took about eighteen and a half hours to arrive. Voyager 2 turned home.

A few months later, Voyager 1 started sending gibberish. One memory chip in its computer had failed. Nobody can replace a chip twenty-four billion kilometers away. So the team moved the code around the dead chip, piece by piece, because no single free spot was big enough to hold it. In April twenty twenty-four, Voyager 1 started making sense again.

And in twenty twenty-five, engineers brought back a set of thrusters that had been considered dead since two thousand four. They gambled, switched them on, and it worked.

Every one of those repairs went the same way. Send a command, then wait almost two days to find out if it worked.

So, why don't we just switch it off?

Each Voyager loses about four watts of power every year. To keep them going, the team turns things off one by one: heaters first, then instruments. On Voyager 1, an instrument that had been running since nineteen seventy-seven was switched off in April twenty twenty-six.

But the two Voyagers are still the only spacecraft taking measurements in interstellar space. Every extra year is data that nobody else can collect, and a new probe would need decades just to get there. So nobody is switching it off. The team keeps it alive for as long as the power lasts.

One day, the power will run out, and Voyager will go quiet on its own. But it won't stop. In about forty thousand years, Voyager 1 will pass less than two light-years from a star called Gliese four four five.

Nobody will be listening by then. But it will still be carrying our music, our pictures, and our hello.
