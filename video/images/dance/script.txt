In July fifteen eighteen, a woman stepped into a street in Strasbourg and started to dance. Nobody knew why. And she didn't stop.

She kept going for four days. Maybe six. Then someone joined her. Then another. By August, somewhere between fifty and four hundred people were dancing in the streets, and they could not stop.

The city's doctors had a diagnosis. Hot blood. And the cure for hot blood, they decided, was more dancing.

So the council cleared out two guild halls and a grain market. They built a wooden stage. They hired musicians to keep the dancers moving.

It made things worse. Later accounts say people collapsed. Some say as many as fifteen a day died. Historians still argue about those numbers.

Finally, the council banned public music and dancing. The dancers were taken to a shrine of Saint Vitus, the saint people believed could curse you with dancing. Many were said to recover there.

So what was it? Poisoned bread is one theory. Historian John Waller has another. Years of famine and disease. And a city that truly believed an angry saint could make you dance. Fear spread. And so did the dance.

It all began with one woman, in a street in Strasbourg, in July fifteen eighteen.
