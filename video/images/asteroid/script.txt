Your city could be wiped out by one of fifteen thousand asteroids nobody has found yet. So why don't we just nuke them before they hit?

That's how the movies do it. Fly up, plant a bomb, and blow the rock to pieces.

The problem is the pieces. Many asteroids are weak, loose piles of rubble. Shatter one, and the danger doesn't disappear. You get a cloud of smaller rocks, and plenty of them can keep heading for Earth.

So scientists prefer a nudge. In 2022, NASA crashed its DART spacecraft into a small asteroid called Dimorphos, at more than fourteen thousand miles an hour. Its orbit got thirty-two minutes shorter. NASA only needed seventy-three seconds to call it a success.

A tiny push is enough, if it comes early. Years before an impact, even a small nudge can turn a hit into a miss. A nuclear blast stays a last resort, for when there's no time left.

And time is the real problem. Of the near-Earth asteroids bigger than a hundred and forty meters, only about forty percent have been found. NASA is building a new space telescope, NEO Surveyor, to hunt for the rest.

The hard part isn't hitting an asteroid. It's seeing it coming.
